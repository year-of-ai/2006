**Key figures**: Fidel Castro, Raúl Castro, Carlos Valenciaga

## Background

Fidel Castro had ruled Cuba since January 1, 1959, when his 26th of July Movement ousted the U.S.-backed Batista government. Over the following 47 years he had survived the Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), repeated U.S. assassination attempts documented by the CIA, and the collapse of the Soviet Union—which devastated Cuba's economy in the "Special Period" of the 1990s. By 2006 he was 79 years old and Cuba's 1976 constitution had already designated his younger brother Raúl, the long-serving defense minister, as his constitutional successor.

In the weeks before July 31, Castro's public appearances had become increasingly infrequent, and speculation about his health circulated internationally, though the Cuban government denied any crisis.

## Summary

On July 31, 2006—the eve of his 80th birthday and Cuba's **26th of July** national holiday—Fidel Castro issued a written proclamation read on Cuban state television by his personal secretary, **Carlos Valenciaga**, announcing that he was temporarily ceding all state, government, and party powers to Raúl Castro. The announcement cited intestinal surgery for acute diverticulitis as the immediate cause, requiring a recovery period of "several weeks."

Raúl Castro assumed command of the Cuban Armed Forces, the Council of State, the Council of Ministers, and the First Secretary position of the Communist Party of Cuba—a full transfer of executive, legislative, and military authority. The initial framing was provisional: Fidel retained his formal titles and continued to send written messages and op-ed columns (known as *Reflexiones*) to the Cuban press. He did not appear in public for months.

George W. Bush's White House responded the same day, calling the transfer "a moment" for the Cuban people to pursue a democratic transition, and convening a special Cabinet-level meeting on Cuba policy. The U.S. Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) maintained strict enforcement of the Cuban embargo throughout, which had been in place since 1962.

## Aftermath

What began as provisional became permanent. Castro made no public appearance until December 2006, when photographs released by the Cuban government showed him receiving Venezuelan President Hugo Chávez in his hospital room—the first visual confirmation of his continued survival. In **February 2008**, Cuba's National Assembly formally elected Raúl Castro as President of the Council of State, institutionalizing the transfer. Fidel formally resigned all party and state titles, though he continued publishing *Reflexiones* until 2016.

Raúl's early governance showed pragmatic continuity. He did not repudiate his brother's revolutionary legacy but introduced limited economic reforms, allowing Cubans to own mobile phones and stay in tourist hotels previously reserved for foreigners. The most significant diplomatic shift came in **December 2014**, when U.S. President Barack Obama and Raúl Castro announced the restoration of diplomatic relations—a development that Fidel acknowledged with cautious skepticism in a brief 2015 public letter.

Fidel Castro died on **November 25, 2016**, at age 90. Raúl stepped down as president in April 2018, handing power to Miguel Díaz-Canel—the first Cuban leader born after the 1959 revolution.

## Significance

Castro's stepping down was among the most geopolitically significant leadership transitions in the Western Hemisphere since the Cold War. It ended one of the longest-running and most internationally scrutinized authoritarian tenures of the 20th century, and raised fundamental questions about whether the Cuban socialist state could survive beyond its founder.

Within Latin America, the transition had cascading implications. Venezuela's Hugo Chávez had become Cuba's principal economic patron through the **Petrocaribe** oil arrangement; Chávez's own health problems (he died in 2013) would later compound Cuban uncertainty. Bolivia's Evo Morales and Ecuador's Rafael Correa—both elected in 2005–2006—represented an ideological cohort that had looked to Castro as a symbolic anchor of the Latin American left.

The 2006 transfer also preceded a wider wave of regional leadership changes. The 2006 U.S. midterm elections shifted congressional power to Democrats, affecting U.S. foreign policy posture. The execution of Saddam Hussein in December 2006 closed another chapter of Cold War-era leadership, and the North Korea nuclear test in October underscored the ongoing relevance of authoritarian states operating outside Western norms.

Castro's health crisis also echoed the near-simultaneous incapacitation of Ariel Sharon, who had suffered a devastating stroke in January 2006—another case of a dominant political figure suddenly removed from power, leaving a successor to navigate an uncertain transition.
