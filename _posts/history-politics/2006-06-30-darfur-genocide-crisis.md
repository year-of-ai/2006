## Summary

The Darfur conflict, which began in 2003 as a regional rebellion against Sudan's government, entered a critical escalation phase in 2006. Despite the May 5 signing of the Darfur Peace Agreement (DPA) between Sudan's government and one rebel faction, widespread rejection of the accord by rival rebel groups and renewed Janjaweed attacks through the summer of 2006 deepened the humanitarian emergency. By mid-2006, cumulative casualties had reached approximately **300,000 to 450,000**, with over **2.8 million people** displaced from their homes. The UN Security Council passed Resolution 1706 on August 31, authorizing 17,300 international peacekeepers — but Sudan's government refused entry, leaving only the understaffed African Union Mission (AMIS) in place. The year marked a turning point at which diplomatic mechanisms, peacekeeping capacity, and international political will were each demonstrably insufficient to halt a state-sponsored humanitarian catastrophe.

## Background

### Origins of the Conflict (2003–2005)

The Darfur conflict originated in **February 2003** when two rebel groups — the Sudan Liberation Movement/Army (SLM/SLA) and the Justice and Equality Movement (JEM) — launched an uprising against Sudan's Arab-dominated government in Khartoum, citing decades of political and economic marginalization of Darfur's predominantly African ethnic groups (primarily the Fur, Masalit, and Zaghawa peoples).

In response, the Sudanese government under President **Omar al-Bashir** mobilized the **Janjaweed** — an Arabic term loosely translating to "horsemen carrying guns" — a paramilitary force composed of armed Arab nomadic tribes who had longstanding land and resource disputes with sedentary African farming communities. Sudanese military aircraft conducted aerial bombardments of villages, followed by Janjaweed cavalry and vehicle-mounted attacks, in a coordinated campaign that produced mass killings, systematic rape, the burning of villages, and the destruction of crops and water sources. The strategy aimed to render areas uninhabitable and displace civilian populations supporting the rebels.

By the end of 2004, the United States Congress had passed a resolution declaring the situation **genocide** — the first time the U.S. government had applied that designation to an ongoing conflict. In 2005, the UN Security Council referred the situation in Darfur to the **International Criminal Court** (ICC) under Resolution 1593, the first such referral in the ICC's history.

### African Union Mission in Sudan (AMIS)

The African Union deployed its peacekeeping mission — the African Union Mission in Sudan (AMIS) — beginning in 2004. By 2006, AMIS comprised approximately **7,000 troops** drawn from Rwanda, Nigeria, Senegal, and other African Union member states. The mission was chronically underfunded, under-equipped, and operating under a mandate that authorized monitoring and observation but not active civilian protection. AMIS troops were forbidden from intervening to protect civilians under attack unless they themselves were threatened — a restriction that repeatedly prevented them from stopping Janjaweed raids. Funding shortfalls periodically delayed salaries and fuel supplies, limiting patrol capacity across an area the size of France.

## The 2006 Crisis

### The Darfur Peace Agreement (May 5, 2006)

The Darfur Peace Agreement (DPA) was signed on **May 5, 2006** in Abuja, Nigeria, after months of negotiations facilitated by the African Union. Sudan's government and the SLA faction led by **Minni Minnawi** signed the 115-page accord, which addressed power-sharing arrangements, the integration of rebel fighters into Sudanese armed forces, a ceasefire mechanism, and wealth redistribution commitments including a **$30 million** compensation fund for conflict victims.

However, the DPA immediately fractured the rebel opposition rather than uniting it:
- The **Justice and Equality Movement (JEM)**, led by Khalil Ibrahim, refused to sign.
- The rival **SLA faction led by Abdul Wahid al-Nur** — which represented the largest rebel constituency and had the most fighters — also refused, citing insufficient guarantees of civilian security and political power-sharing.

The two non-signatory factions collectively represented more combatants than Minnawi's signatory group. Their rejection rendered the accord politically invalid as a peace framework. Within weeks, fighting between Minnawi's now-government-allied forces and the non-signatory factions added a new intra-rebel dimension to the violence, further complicating humanitarian operations.

### Renewed Violence and Humanitarian Deterioration (Summer 2006)

Through July and August 2006, violence in Darfur intensified sharply. Documented incidents included:

- Janjaweed attacks on the **Kalma refugee camp** — one of the largest displacement sites in Darfur — where the International Rescue Committee documented that **hundreds of women were raped** over several weeks in a systematic campaign against camp residents.
- Aerial bombardments of villages by Sudanese government aircraft in North Darfur.
- Attacks on humanitarian workers: the UN reported **26 aid workers killed** in Darfur in the first eight months of 2006.
- Food access blockages affecting an estimated **355,000 people** in conflict-affected areas, according to the UN World Food Programme.

UN Secretary-General **Kofi Annan** warned publicly of an impending "humanitarian catastrophe" as conditions deteriorated. Jan Egeland, the UN's top humanitarian official, described the situation as potentially "the world's greatest humanitarian crisis."

### Cumulative Death and Displacement Toll

Calculating precise casualty figures in Darfur was itself a contested process — access restrictions prevented systematic surveys, and different methodologies produced divergent results. By mid-2006:

- The **U.S. Government Accountability Office** and State Department estimated **200,000 to 400,000 deaths** attributable to the conflict since 2003, from a combination of direct violence and conflict-induced disease and malnutrition.
- The **UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs** estimated **2.5 to 3 million internally displaced persons** within Darfur.
- An estimated **234,000 Darfuri refugees** had crossed into **Chad**, straining that country's own humanitarian capacity and provoking instability along the Chadian-Sudanese border, where Janjaweed pursued refugees across international boundaries.

## International Response

### UN Security Council Resolution 1706 (August 31, 2006)

On **August 31, 2006**, the UN Security Council passed **Resolution 1706** by a vote of 12-0 with three abstentions (China, Russia, and Qatar). The resolution authorized expanding the UN Mission in Sudan (UNMIS) by **17,300 peacekeepers and 3,300 police** to replace AMIS's overstretched force, with a mandate explicitly including civilian protection.

Sudan's government immediately and forcefully rejected the deployment. President Omar al-Bashir condemned it as "colonialism" and threatened military resistance to any UN force entering Sudan without his government's consent. The government demanded that AMIS withdraw by **September 30, 2006**, a demand the African Union ultimately did not fulfill, but the confrontation underscored the fundamental contradiction: the Security Council could authorize a peacekeeping force but could not deploy it against the host government's will without invoking Chapter VII enforcement action, which China and Russia had signaled they would veto.

### U.S. Congressional and Executive Response

In the United States, Darfur had become an unusual bipartisan cause, mobilized in part by activist organizations including the **Save Darfur Coalition** (founded 2004), which organized the "Million Voices for Darfur" postcard campaign and rallied constituencies including evangelical Christians, college students, and Jewish community organizations.

Congress passed the **Darfur Peace and Accountability Act** in September 2006, signed by President George W. Bush on **October 13, 2006**. The Act:
- Imposed targeted financial sanctions and asset freezes on individuals identified as responsible for atrocities in Darfur.
- Called for assistance to the ICC's investigation.
- Urged the Administration to work toward a UN peacekeeping force with civilian protection mandate.

The Bush administration simultaneously pursued diplomatic pressure through the State Department, with Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice designating Darfur **"genocide"** — a designation first made by Secretary Colin Powell in 2004 and maintained by the administration.

### ICC Investigation and Accountability

The **International Criminal Court** had opened its Darfur investigation in **June 2005**, following the Security Council's referral under Resolution 1593. In 2006, ICC Prosecutor **Luis Moreno Ocampo** continued evidence-gathering amid severe access constraints — Sudan refused to cooperate with ICC investigators. The investigation focused on identifying individuals responsible for crimes against humanity and war crimes, with genocide charges under consideration.

The Darfur ICC process was significant as the first investigation arising from a Security Council referral, testing whether the international community's accountability framework could function when the alleged perpetrators governed a sovereign state. The investigation would culminate in **March 2009** when Moreno Ocampo sought — and the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber granted — an arrest warrant for President Omar al-Bashir on charges of war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. This was the first ICC arrest warrant issued for a sitting head of state.

The backdrop of international criminal accountability in 2006 also included the trial and death of **Slobodan Milošević** at the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) — see [Slobodan Milošević]({{ '/news/people/slobodan-milosevic/' | relative_url }}) — which set a precedent of holding heads of state criminally liable for atrocity crimes, a precedent the Darfur investigation sought to build upon.

### The Responsibility to Protect Debate

The 2006 Darfur escalation became the first major test of the **Responsibility to Protect (R2P)** doctrine, which had been unanimously endorsed by the UN World Summit in September 2005. R2P held that when states failed to protect their populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity, the international community had a responsibility to intervene — including through coercive measures if necessary.

Sudan's intransigent refusal of UN peacekeepers while atrocities continued exposed R2P's limits: the doctrine had been agreed in principle but lacked enforcement mechanisms capable of overcoming the great-power politics of the Security Council (where China, with oil investments in Sudan, and Russia resisted strong action). The 2006 Darfur crisis thus crystallized the gap between the humanitarian intervention norm articulated in 2005 and the institutional capacity to act on it.

## Significance

The 2006 escalation was a watershed moment in international humanitarian crisis response. It exposed the inadequacy of existing peacekeeping and diplomatic mechanisms when confronted with systematic mass atrocities and a sovereign government's refusal to cooperate. Three specific failures stood out:

1. **Peacekeeping without consent**: AMIS demonstrated that consent-based peacekeeping could not protect civilians from the very government providing that consent.
2. **Peace agreements without buy-in**: The DPA showed that a peace accord signed by only one rebel faction while others continued fighting could not stabilize a conflict.
3. **Resolutions without deployment**: Resolution 1706 demonstrated that UN authorization without the means or will to enforce deployment was largely symbolic.

The 2006 failure catalyzed the eventual establishment of the **UN-African Union Mission in Darfur (UNAMID)** in 2007 — the first hybrid UN-AU peacekeeping force — which was authorized under Security Council Resolution 1769 on July 31, 2007. Even UNAMID, however, faced chronic access restrictions and operational limitations throughout its deployment (2007–2020).

The Darfur crisis of 2006 unfolded alongside other instances of UN Security Council crisis management that year, including the [2006 Lebanon War]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-lebanon-war/' | relative_url }}) and [North Korea's first nuclear test]({{ '/news/history-politics/north-korea-nuclear-test/' | relative_url }}), each of which tested the Security Council's credibility and cohesion. The eventual execution of Saddam Hussein in December 2006 — see [Saddam Hussein Execution]({{ '/news/history-politics/saddam-hussein-execution/' | relative_url }}) — underscored the contested state of international law and sovereignty norms that framed all these crises simultaneously.

In the United States, the Darfur crisis and Congressional activism around it contributed to the political environment leading into the [2006 midterm elections]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-us-midterm-elections/' | relative_url }}), in which opposition Democrats ran partly on critiques of the Bush administration's foreign policy choices — including what critics characterized as an insufficient response to Darfur while pursuing costly wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.
