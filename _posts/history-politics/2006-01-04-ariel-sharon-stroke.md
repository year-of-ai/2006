## Summary

Ariel Sharon suffered a massive hemorrhagic stroke on January 4, 2006, at his Sycamore Ranch in the Negev desert while serving as Prime Minister of Israel. Sharon, who had been born on February 26, 1928, and was 77 years old at the time, had been hospitalized on December 18, 2005, following a mild ischemic stroke and was undergoing anticoagulant treatment for a subsequently discovered heart defect when the catastrophic cerebral hemorrhage occurred.

Rushed by helicopter to Hadassah Medical Center in Jerusalem, Sharon underwent more than 21 hours of emergency surgeries to relieve pressure on his brain. Despite the intervention, he never regained consciousness. Medical teams placed him in a medically induced coma, and he was transferred to the long-term care facility at Sheba Medical Center near Tel Aviv in April 2006, where he remained in a persistent vegetative state until his death on January 11, 2014 — eight years after the stroke.

## Political Context

### Sharon's Career and the Founding of Kadima

Ariel Sharon was one of the most consequential and controversial figures in Israeli history. He had served as a military commander since the 1948 Arab-Israeli War, founded and led the elite Unit 101 paratroopers in the early 1950s, commanded armored forces in the 1967 Six-Day War and the 1973 Yom Kippur War, and served in multiple cabinet roles including Agriculture Minister and Defense Minister — during which his indirect responsibility for the Sabra and Shatila massacre in 1982 triggered a national inquiry and his temporary removal from the defense portfolio.

Elected Prime Minister in February 2001 as a Likud candidate, Sharon underwent a remarkable political transformation over the following years. His decision to implement a unilateral Israeli withdrawal from the Gaza Strip — completed in August 2005 after evacuating approximately 8,500 settlers from 21 settlements — stunned political observers who had expected the hawkish Sharon to oppose any such disengagement. Facing fierce internal opposition from Likud hardliners, Sharon resigned from Likud in November 2005 and founded a new centrist party, Kadima ("Forward"), which he intended to lead into the scheduled March 2006 elections.

### Timing of the Stroke

The stroke struck at an exceptionally sensitive moment in Israeli politics. Sharon had assembled an unprecedented centrist coalition around Kadima within weeks of the party's founding, drawing senior figures from both Likud and Labor. Opinion polls in late 2005 and early January 2006 consistently showed Kadima leading with roughly 40 seats in the 120-seat Knesset — a margin that would have given Sharon the dominant position in any coalition. Shimon Peres, a former Prime Minister and Nobel Peace Prize laureate, had defected from Labor to Kadima, lending the new party cross-ideological credibility.

The stroke also occurred just 21 days before the Palestinian legislative elections scheduled for January 25, 2006 — elections in which Hamas would unexpectedly defeat Fatah and win a governing majority in the Palestinian Authority (see [2006 Palestinian Legislative Election]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-palestinian-legislative-election/' | relative_url }})), dramatically altering the landscape of Israeli-Palestinian relations precisely as Israel faced a leadership vacuum.

## Transfer of Power

### Ehud Olmert as Acting Prime Minister

Under Israeli Basic Law, the Vice Prime Minister assumes executive authority when the Prime Minister is incapacitated. Ehud Olmert, who had served as Mayor of Jerusalem and joined Kadima when Sharon founded it, had been designated Vice Prime Minister. He assumed the role of Acting Prime Minister on January 4, 2006, hours after Sharon's emergency hospitalization.

On April 11, 2006, the Israeli Cabinet formally declared Sharon permanently incapacitated — a step delayed for more than three months out of both medical uncertainty and political caution. On April 14, 2006, Olmert was formally elevated to Interim Prime Minister. Kadima went on to win the March 28, 2006 Knesset elections, securing 29 seats (reduced from the 40+ pre-stroke polls had projected, partly reflecting public uncertainty about the party without its founder). Olmert negotiated a coalition government and was sworn in as Prime Minister on May 4, 2006, with Labor's Amir Peretz as Defense Minister and Shimon Peres as a senior minister.

## Significance

### Domestic Political Realignment

Sharon's stroke foreclosed a political trajectory that had seemed poised to reshape Israeli domestic politics around a durable centrist majority. Olmert inherited leadership of a party whose identity had been built almost entirely around its founder's personal authority and whose policy directions — continued unilateral territorial steps, opposition to Hamas, quiet diplomacy — were now being executed by a far less experienced executive. Political analysts noted that Kadima under Olmert lacked the strategic credibility Sharon had accumulated through five decades of military and political service.

### The Second Lebanon War

The most immediate test of the transition came with the outbreak of the Second Lebanon War on July 12, 2006, when Hezbollah militants crossed into northern Israel, killed eight soldiers, and abducted two others in a cross-border raid. Olmert's decision to launch a major military campaign against Hezbollah in Lebanon, lasting 34 days and resulting in significant Israeli civilian and military casualties alongside massive destruction in southern Lebanon, was widely criticized as poorly planned and executed. A government commission (the Winograd Commission) later sharply rebuked Olmert's wartime decision-making. Observers frequently speculated that the outcome might have differed under Sharon's command, though counterfactual assessments varied (see [Second Lebanon War]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-lebanon-war/' | relative_url }})).

### Sharon's Long Absence and Death

Sharon's prolonged vegetative state from January 2006 through January 2014 — an unprecedented eight-year period of incapacitation for a sitting head of government — meant that one of the defining figures in Israeli and Middle Eastern history was entirely absent during a period that included the Second Lebanon War, the 2008–09 Gaza War, the Arab Spring, and multiple Israeli–Palestinian negotiations. His death on January 11, 2014, reopened debates about what his political trajectory might have yielded had he survived.
