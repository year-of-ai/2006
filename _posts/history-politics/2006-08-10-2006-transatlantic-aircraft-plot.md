## Summary

The **2006 transatlantic aircraft plot** was a terrorist conspiracy to detonate **liquid explosives** aboard several airliners travelling from the United Kingdom to the United States and Canada, disguised as soft drinks. British police, in a surveillance operation code-named **Operation Overt**, arrested **24 suspects** in and around London, Birmingham, and High Wycombe on the night of **9 August 2006**, and the disruption of the plot was announced publicly on **10 August 2006**. The plotters intended to carry a hydrogen-peroxide–based explosive in modified soft-drink bottles and to assemble and detonate the devices in flight. Britain's **Joint Terrorism Analysis Centre** raised the national threat level to its highest rank, **"Critical,"** on 10 August, and airports across the UK and US imposed an immediate near-total ban on liquids in carry-on baggage. Although the emergency measures were eased within weeks, they hardened into the enduring worldwide restriction limiting cabin liquids to containers of **100 millilitres (3.4 US fl oz)**—one of the most visible and lasting changes to global air travel of the early twenty-first century. Following trials between 2008 and 2010, several of the plotters received life sentences.

## Background

The plot was planned in the United Kingdom by a group of British men, most of them from London and the surrounding area, and was investigated over months by the Metropolitan Police and the Security Service (MI5) under the code name **Operation Overt**. The alleged ringleader was **Abdulla Ahmed Ali** of Walthamstow, east London, working with **Assad Sarwar**, who acquired and processed the chemicals, and **Tanvir Hussain**. Investigators later said the conspirators had recorded "martyrdom videos" at a flat in Walthamstow, indicating an intended suicide operation. British and US officials alleged the plot was connected to **al-Qaeda's** leadership in Pakistan, with a Birmingham-born man, **Rashid Rauf**, said to have acted as the intermediary; the timing of the August arrests was reported to have been influenced by Rauf's detention in Pakistan.

## The Plot

The conspirators planned to smuggle the components of the bombs aboard aircraft separately and assemble them in flight. The main charge was to be a **hydrogen-peroxide–based liquid explosive** concealed inside **500 ml plastic bottles** of soft drinks such as Oasis and Lucozade, with the bottles emptied and refilled so as to appear factory-sealed. A detonator was to be hidden in the hollowed-out casing of an **AA battery** and triggered using the circuitry of a **disposable camera's flash unit**. Because the components individually resembled ordinary consumer items and liquids were then permitted through security, the method was designed to evade the screening in place at the time. Court proceedings indicated the plotters had identified several transatlantic flights from **London Heathrow** to US and Canadian cities—operated by carriers including United Airlines, American Airlines, and Air Canada—departing within a short window of one another, so that multiple aircraft could be brought down almost simultaneously.

## Security Response

The response to the arrests was immediate and severe. On **10 August 2006** the UK threat level was raised to **"Critical,"** signalling that an attack was believed to be imminent, and British airports banned virtually all liquids and hand baggage, causing widespread flight cancellations and long delays at Heathrow and other hubs. In the United States, the Department of Homeland Security **banned all liquids and gels** in carry-on luggage, exempting only baby formula and prescription medication. The UK threat level was reduced to **"Severe"** on **14 August 2006**, and the blanket prohibitions were progressively replaced by calibrated limits:

- **26 September 2006** — the US Transportation Security Administration introduced the **"3-1-1" rule**, permitting liquids in containers of **3.4 ounces (100 ml) or less** carried in a single quart-size clear bag.
- **October–November 2006** — the European Union and the UK adopted comparable **100 ml** limits for cabin liquids.

Countries across Asia and the rest of the world followed with the same restriction, and the **100 ml carry-on liquids rule** became a permanent global aviation-security standard that remained in force for years afterward.

## Trials and Outcome

Of the 24 people arrested, eight were eventually tried for the core conspiracy. A first trial ran from **April to September 2008**; the jury convicted three men—**Abdulla Ahmed Ali, Assad Sarwar,** and **Tanvir Hussain**—of conspiracy to murder but did not reach a verdict on the specific charge of conspiring to blow up aircraft. At a **retrial in September 2009**, Ali, Sarwar, and Hussain were convicted of **conspiracy to murder by detonating bombs aboard aircraft** and sentenced to life imprisonment, with Ali ordered to serve a minimum of **40 years**, Sarwar a minimum of **36 years**, and Hussain a minimum of **32 years**; the trial judge described the plot as among the gravest ever proven in a British court. Further convictions of additional conspirators followed in **2010**. Rashid Rauf, the alleged al-Qaeda contact, was never tried: he escaped from custody in Pakistan in 2007 and was reported to have been killed in a US drone strike in 2008.

## Significance

The 2006 transatlantic aircraft plot was one of the most consequential counterterrorism episodes of its year, both for the scale of the attack it was intended to inflict—potentially rivalling the September 11, 2001 attacks in casualties had it succeeded—and for the durability of its aftermath. Its most far-reaching legacy was the worldwide restriction on liquids in carry-on baggage, which touched hundreds of millions of travellers annually and became, for a generation of passengers, the defining ritual of airport security. The plot demonstrated the continuing threat of al-Qaeda–linked networks operating within Western countries five years after 9/11, reinforced the intelligence relationship between British and American security services, and drove sustained investment in explosives-detection technology. Coming in the same summer as the [2006 Lebanon War]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-lebanon-war/' | relative_url }}) and amid the sectarian violence following the [al-Askari Mosque bombing]({{ '/news/history-politics/al-askari-mosque-bombing/' | relative_url }}), it underscored 2006 as a year in which the global security architecture built after 2001 was repeatedly tested.
