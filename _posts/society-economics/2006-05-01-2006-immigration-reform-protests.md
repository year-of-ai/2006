## Summary

The **2006 U.S. immigration-reform protests** were a wave of mass demonstrations across the United States between February and May 2006, mobilizing an estimated **3.5 to 5 million people** in more than **140 cities** — by most accounts the largest coordinated protest movement in American history to that point. The marches were triggered by **H.R. 4437**, the Border Protection, Anti-terrorism, and Illegal Immigration Control Act, which had passed the House of Representatives on **December 16, 2005**, by a vote of **239–182**.

Predominantly Latino, the protests demanded a path to legalization rather than criminalization, and drew unprecedented support from immigrant communities, the Catholic Church, labor unions, and Spanish-language media. They peaked on **May 1, 2006** — dubbed the **"Day Without Immigrants"** or the "Great American Boycott" — when between **1.6 and 2.2 million people** marched and many stayed home from work and school to demonstrate immigrants' economic weight.

## Background: H.R. 4437

Sponsored by Wisconsin Representative **F. James Sensenbrenner**, H.R. 4437 would have made unlawful presence in the United States a **felony** (rather than a civil violation), imposed new penalties on anyone who assisted undocumented immigrants — potentially including clergy and aid workers — and mandated hundreds of miles of border fencing. The bill's felony provision and its criminalization of humanitarian assistance galvanized immigrant communities and religious leaders. **Cardinal Roger Mahony** of Los Angeles publicly vowed that the Church would defy the law, and Spanish-language broadcasters, including radio host **Eddie "Piolín" Sotelo** and the networks Univision and Telemundo, amplified calls to march.

## The marches

The movement built through the spring in a rising sequence of demonstrations:

- **March 10, 2006 — Chicago:** roughly 100,000 people (police estimate), the first of the season's major marches.
- **March 25, 2006 — Los Angeles ("La Gran Marcha"):** an official estimate of more than **500,000**, with organizers claiming well over a million — the single largest protest of the movement.
- **April 10, 2006 — National Day of Action:** coordinated marches in **102 cities**, drawing hundreds of thousands, including very large crowds in Dallas, Washington, and New York.
- **May 1, 2006 — "A Day Without Immigrants":** a nationwide strike, boycott, and set of marches involving an estimated **1.6–2.2 million** participants, including some 400,000 in Chicago and 400,000 in Los Angeles.

## Outcome

The protests helped defeat H.R. 4437: the bill **failed to advance in the Senate**, and a rival Senate measure offering a path to legalization (S. 2611) never survived conference with the House. No comprehensive immigration overhaul became law in 2006; instead Congress passed the narrower **Secure Fence Act**, authorizing additional border barriers, which President George W. Bush signed in October 2006.

Immigration remained a charged issue heading into the November 2006 elections. The demonstrations energized both immigrant-advocacy networks and their opponents — the Minuteman Project reported a sharp rise in interest — and the split within the Republican Party over enforcement versus a guest-worker compromise became one of the cross-currents of the [2006 U.S. midterm elections]({{ '/news/history-politics/2006-us-midterm-elections/' | relative_url }}), which returned control of Congress to the Democrats.

## Significance

The 2006 marches marked the political coming-of-age of the modern U.S. immigrant-rights movement. They demonstrated an unprecedented capacity for Latino and immigrant communities to mobilize at national scale, converted a technical legislative fight into a mass civic event, and established May 1 as a recurring day of immigrant mobilization in subsequent years. They also foreshadowed the durable stalemate over comprehensive immigration reform: the same alignment of forces that stopped H.R. 4437 could not, over the following two decades, produce a legalization framework, leaving immigration one of the most persistent unresolved questions in American politics.
