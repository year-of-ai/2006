## Summary

*The Da Vinci Code*, directed by Ron Howard and adapted by Akiva Goldsman from Dan Brown's 2003 bestselling novel of the same name, premiered at the Cannes Film Festival on May 17, 2006, and opened in wide theatrical release on May 19, 2006. The film follows Robert Langdon (Tom Hanks), a Harvard symbologist, who becomes entangled in a murder investigation at the Louvre Museum in Paris when the museum's curator is found dead. Accompanied by Sophie Neveu (Audrey Tautou), the curator's cryptographer granddaughter, Langdon embarks on a cross-European quest involving art, history, religious symbology, and conspiracy theories surrounding Jesus Christ, Mary Magdalene, and the Holy Grail.

The supporting ensemble includes Sir Ian McKellen as the charming historian Leigh Teabing, who becomes central to the mystery, Jean Reno as a French police captain pursuing them, and Paul Bettany as Silas, a controversial figure connected to the quasi-historical Catholic organization Opus Dei. The production budget was $125 million, making it one of the year's most expensive productions. The screenplay condensed and streamlined Brown's labyrinthine plot to fit a 149-minute runtime, necessarily omitting subplots and compressing the theological arguments that fueled the novel's controversy.

Dan Brown's source novel had sold more than 60 million copies worldwide by 2006, making it the best-selling mystery novel of all time and generating a cultural phenomenon that had already spawned academic conferences, documentary films, and theological rebuttals before Howard's adaptation reached theatres.

## Box Office and Production

The film grossed $758.2 million worldwide against its $125 million production budget—ranking it the second-highest-grossing film of 2006 worldwide, behind only [Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Man's Chest]({{ '/news/arts-culture/pirates-of-the-caribbean-dead-mans-chest/' | relative_url }}). Domestically, it earned $217.5 million. Its opening weekend of $77.1 million set a record for a film opening in May (at the time), demonstrating both the brand power of Brown's novel and the appeal of the star-studded cast.

Principal photography took place across France, England, and Scotland, including filming inside actual locations such as the Louvre in Paris and Lincoln Cathedral (standing in for Westminster Abbey). Access to filming inside the Louvre was partly negotiated due to the French government's cooperation with productions celebrating French culture and tourism; the Lincoln Cathedral exterior substituted for locations the film's producers could not secure inside Westminster.

The Cannes premiere drew a mixed reception from critics but enormous press coverage, with theological protesters outside the Palais des Festivals forming a backdrop that itself became international news.

## Religious Controversy

The film's release coincided with—and amplified—sustained global religious controversy. The Catholic League in the United States issued formal protests against the film's premise, arguing it misrepresented Church history. Several countries, including India, Sri Lanka, Egypt, Pakistan, and the Philippines, imposed partial or full bans on the film's screening. The Vatican issued a statement urging Catholics to boycott the film and the novel alike, calling the work "a blatant lie."

Ironically, the controversy functioned as a marketing asset. Studios reported that protest coverage generated hundreds of millions of dollars of equivalent advertising spend, driving audience curiosity and ensuring broad media attention in markets where mainstream thriller films would not otherwise receive significant coverage.

## Critical Reception

Reviews were mixed but widely published. On Rotten Tomatoes, the film holds a 25% approval rating from critics but a significantly higher 57% audience score, demonstrating the gap between professional critical evaluation and popular reception. Admirers praised Howard's direction, the production design anchored by production designer Allan Cameron, and the film's successful translation of a complex novel's narrative structure into coherent cinema. Detractors criticized the film's plodding pace, Hanks' subdued performance, and the limited chemistry between leads. The Cannes press screening reportedly drew laughter and derision during some dramatic moments, which was reported internationally and hurt early critical credibility.

Roger Ebert awarded the film two and a half stars out of four, calling it "a solid and sometimes engrossing film, but not a great one," while noting that Howard handled difficult material with professionalism even if the result felt mechanical.

## Significance

*The Da Vinci Code* film represented the apex of the novel's cultural dominance and expanded its influence beyond literary circles into mainstream visual media. Commercially, the film's success established a model for adapting dense, plot-heavy literary bestsellers into blockbuster cinema, a template studios pursued aggressively through the late 2000s. Tom Hanks' casting as Langdon proved successful enough to warrant a sequel (*Angels & Demons*, 2009) and a third installment (*Inferno*, 2016), demonstrating the franchise's viability even as each sequel performed below its predecessor.

The film also contributed to a wider cultural interest in art-historical mysteries, encoded symbolism in Renaissance painting, and the history of religious institutions—subjects that generated their own wave of books, television documentaries, and museum exhibition programming between 2006 and 2010.

Its release in the same summer as other major 2006 blockbusters—including [Superman Returns]({{ '/news/arts-culture/superman-returns/' | relative_url }}) and [Pirates of the Caribbean: Dead Man's Chest]({{ '/news/arts-culture/pirates-of-the-caribbean-dead-mans-chest/' | relative_url }})—positioned it within a competitive landscape of franchise-anchored spectacle cinema, yet it stood apart as a literary adaptation rather than a comic-book or sequel property.
