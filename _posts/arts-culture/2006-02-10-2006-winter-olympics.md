## Summary

The 2006 Winter Olympics, officially the **XX Olympic Winter Games**, were held in Turin (**Torino** in Italian), Italy, from **February 10 to 26, 2006**. Some **2,508 athletes** from **80 National Olympic Committees** competed in **84 events** across 15 disciplines grouped into 7 sports, with Albania, Ethiopia, and Madagascar making their Winter Olympic debuts. It was Italy's second time hosting the Winter Games, after Cortina d'Ampezzo in 1956, and events were staged across venues in Turin and the surrounding Alpine valleys of Piedmont.

Germany topped the medal table, winning **11 gold medals and 29 medals overall**. The United States finished second with 9 golds and 25 total medals, and Austria third with 9 golds and 23 total. The Games were overshadowed by a doping scandal centered on the Austrian team, in which Italian police seized blood-transfusion equipment from team lodgings; six Austrian athletes were subsequently banned.

## Turin as Host City

Turin was selected as host in a vote at the IOC Session in **Sion, Switzerland, in June 1999**, defeating Sion (Switzerland), Helsinki, Klagenfurt, Poprad-Tatry, and Zakopane. The choice continued a pattern of major European cities — Salt Lake City (2002), Athens (summer, 2004) — hosting Olympic events, and reflected Turin's status as a major industrial city (home to Fiat's headquarters) seeking to rebrand through international sporting prestige.

The Games' official cost was approximately **€2.2 billion** (roughly $2.7 billion), though independent estimates placed the total public expenditure — including infrastructure, transport improvements, and legacy facilities — closer to **€3.5–4 billion**. The IOC reported that the Games produced a net positive impact for the region, but Italian government auditors later noted that several post-Games venues were underutilized, a common criticism of Winter Olympic infrastructure in cities without a large existing ski-sport culture.

The opening ceremony was held at the **Stadio Olimpico** and featured Yoko Ono as a peace ambassador, symbolizing the Games' "Passion Lives Here" motto; Italian tenor **Andrea Bocelli** performed.

## Key Athletic Performances

### Figure Skating

**Shizuka Arakawa** of Japan won the women's figure skating gold medal with a composed performance featuring a standout layback Ina Bauer — a move widely credited with winning the gold — becoming the **first Japanese athlete to win an Olympic figure skating gold medal** and only the third Asian woman in Olympic figure skating history to win gold. Russia's **Irina Slutskaya**, a six-time world champion, finished third.

In men's figure skating, Russia's **Evgeni Plushenko** won gold after setting a **world-record short-program score of 90.66 points**, demonstrating quad-heavy technical dominance. American **Johnny Weir** finished fifth; the competition highlighted the debate between technical content and artistic expression that would later divide the sport.

The pairs and ice dance competitions were dominated by Russia and the United States respectively, with **Tatiana Totmianina and Maxim Marinin** taking pairs gold and **Tanith Belbin and Ben Agosto** winning ice dance silver for the U.S. — the first American ice dance medal in 30 years.

### Speed Skating

Canada's **Cindy Klassen** was the most decorated athlete of the Games, winning **five medals** (1 gold, 2 silver, 2 bronze) across multiple speed skating distances, becoming the first Canadian to win five medals in a single Winter Games. Her gold came in the 1,500-metre event on February 18.

South Korea's **Ahn Hyun-soo** (also known as Viktor Ahn, later competing for Russia) dominated short-track speed skating with three gold medals (500m, 1,000m, and 5,000m relay), while compatriot **Jin Sun-yu** won the women's 1,000m and 1,500m, also taking three golds including the relay.

### Alpine Skiing and Biathlon

Germany's **Michael Greis** won three biathlon golds (7.5km sprint, 4×7.5km relay, and mass start), contributing to Germany's overall medal-table dominance. Austria's **Benjamin Raich** won alpine skiing golds in the giant slalom and combined events. American **Bode Miller**, despite enormous pre-Games media expectations (he had won the overall World Cup in 2005), failed to win any medal in Turin after disqualifications and poor results — a result that became a widely-discussed example of premature sports media hype.

Skeleton gold went to Canada's **Duff Gibson** at age **39**, making him the **oldest individual gold medalist in Winter Olympic history** to that point.

## The Austrian Doping Scandal

The Games' most significant controversy was a doping operation targeting Austria's cross-country skiing and biathlon teams. On **February 18, 2006**, Italian police — acting on intelligence from the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) and information that **Walter Mayer**, a former Austrian coach who had been banned from Olympic venues for three years following doping allegations at the 2002 Salt Lake City Games, was present in the Austrian team facilities — raided the team's lodgings in Pragelato. They seized **blood-transfusion equipment** and doping paraphernalia, and found Mayer on the premises.

In-competition drug tests of the athletes present returned **negative** results immediately, but the IOC and Austrian Olympic Committee (ÖOC) investigated further. Six Austrian athletes were ultimately identified and sanctioned:

| Athlete | Sport | Sanction |
|---|---|---|
| Wolfgang Perner | Biathlon | 2-year ban |
| Wolfgang Rottmann | Biathlon | 2-year ban |
| Martin Tauber | Cross-country skiing | 2-year ban |
| Jürgen Pinter | Cross-country skiing | 2-year ban |
| Johannes Eder | Cross-country skiing | 2-year ban |
| Roland Diethart | Cross-country skiing | 2-year ban |

The Austrian Olympic Committee was fined **$1 million** by the IOC. Walter Mayer was banned from Olympic participation for life. The scandal reinforced the growing evidence that **blood doping** (autologous blood transfusions, EPO) was widespread in endurance sports, and it helped build the political will for more aggressive testing and the Biological Passport program that WADA introduced from 2008.

## Significance

As one of the largest recurring global sporting events, the Turin Games drew a worldwide television audience and stood alongside that summer's [FIFA World Cup]({{ '/news/arts-culture/fifa-world-cup-2006/' | relative_url }}) as one of 2006's two marquee international sporting spectacles hosted by Italy. Arakawa's victory was a landmark for Japanese winter sport, and the dominance of German athletes in the traditional Nordic and biathlon disciplines reaffirmed central Europe's sustained strength in winter competition.

The Austrian doping raid was among the most consequential Olympic anti-doping actions of its era, demonstrating a new willingness by host-nation authorities to use police powers against organized doping — a precedent that contributed to the later aggressive enforcement approach adopted at Sochi 2014 (where a Russian state-sponsored doping program was later exposed) and beyond. The Games also illustrated the escalating cost burden of Olympic hosting: the €2.2 billion+ expenditure for a 16-day event contributed to growing IOC discussion of hosting reform that culminated in the *Agenda 2020* reforms adopted in 2014.
