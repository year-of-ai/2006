# 2006 Asian Games in Doha

**Category:** Arts & Culture

**Key figures:** Sheikh Ahmad Al-Fahad Al-Sabah (OCA President), Qatar Olympic Committee (host), China's delegation (dominant medal performance), India's athletics contingent (record national haul), Kosuke Kitajima (Japan, swimming double gold)

## Summary

The XV Asian Games — officially the Doha 2006 Asian Games — took place in Doha, Qatar, from December 1 to December 15, 2006. Organised by the Olympic Council of Asia (OCA) and hosted by the Qatar Olympic Committee, the Games were the first Asian Games held in the Middle East and the largest multi-sport event the region had staged to that point. Some 9,520 athletes from all 45 OCA member nations competed across 39 sports (46 disciplines) in 424 events, making Doha 2006 the most expansive edition of the Asian Games to date.

Qatar invested an estimated $2.8 billion USD in infrastructure and venues — a figure unprecedented for the Games — including the Khalifa International Stadium (refurbished), the Hamad Aquatic Centre, and the Al-Rayyan Indoor Arena. The country used the event as a deliberate showcase of its capacity to host global sports, laying groundwork for its successful bid to host the 2022 FIFA World Cup.

## Host Nation and Preparations

Qatar's bid to host the XV Asiad had been awarded in 2000, giving the country six years to build or refurbish the required venues. The Aspire Zone (Khalifa Sport City complex) served as the hub of competition, encompassing:

- **Khalifa International Stadium** — the main athletics venue and opening/closing ceremony site, capacity approximately 50,000
- **Hamad Aquatic Centre** — purpose-built for swimming and water polo
- **Al-Sadd Sports Club** — football (soccer) group-stage venue
- **Multipurpose Indoor Hall** — combat sports, gymnastics, table tennis

Doha's summer heat precluded a June–September window, which had been traditional for Asian Games. The December scheduling — with temperatures averaging 20–25 °C during competition — set a precedent for the 2022 FIFA World Cup, also moved to November–December for the same climatic reason.

## Competition and Medal Table

China dominated the overall medal standings, winning 165 gold medals and 407 total medals, extending the nation's unbroken run of Asian Games supremacy that had continued since 1982. South Korea finished second with 58 gold medals, and Japan third with 50 gold medals.

**Top five nations, 2006 Asian Games:**

| Nation | Gold | Silver | Bronze | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| China | 165 | 88 | 63 | 316 |
| South Korea | 58 | 53 | 82 | 193 |
| Japan | 50 | 71 | 77 | 198 |
| Kazakhstan | 23 | 20 | 30 | 73 |
| India | 10 | 17 | 26 | 53 |

India's haul of 53 medals (10 gold) was significant given the nation's historically modest Asian Games performance outside of wrestling and shooting. Shooter Abhinav Bindra and boxers contributed to the tally, and the performance was cited in subsequent Indian sports policy discussions as evidence that structured funding could deliver results.

## Notable Performances

**Swimming — Kosuke Kitajima (Japan):** The 2004 Athens Olympic breaststroke double-gold medallist repeated his feat in Doha, winning the 100m and 200m breaststroke events and setting Games records in both. His dominance in breaststroke was a defining feature of mid-2000s Asian aquatics.

**Athletics — Liu Xiang (China):** The 110m hurdles world record holder and 2004 Olympic champion won gold in his signature event in Doha, reinforcing his status as the centrepiece of Chinese track-and-field ambition.

**Weightlifting — Hossein Rezazadeh (Iran):** The two-time Olympic super-heavyweight champion competed at the Games, with Iran finishing among the top ten nations in weightlifting.

**Football (Soccer):** Japan's men's team won gold. Women's football featured China's national team.

**Kabaddi:** India won the men's kabaddi gold, extending the nation's unbeaten streak in the sport at the Asian Games. (A women's kabaddi competition was not added to the Asian Games programme until Guangzhou 2010.)

## Significance

The Doha 2006 Asian Games established Qatar's credibility as a large-scale sporting host and provided proof-of-concept for staging a major international event in the Gulf. The $2.8 billion infrastructure investment — including roads, venues, and athlete accommodation — left a lasting physical legacy used in Qatar's 2022 FIFA World Cup preparations. The December scheduling, mandated by climate, became a model for future Gulf sporting events.

For the OCA, the Games demonstrated the organisation's willingness to expand beyond East and Southeast Asia toward the Middle East and Central Asian membership, reflecting shifts in OCA political influence toward Gulf states. The Doha framework influenced subsequent bids from Gulf cities for IOC recognition and global multi-sport hosting.
