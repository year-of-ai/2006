## Summary

**Slobodan Milošević**, the former President of Serbia (1991–1997) and of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (1997–2000), died on **11 March 2006** at age **64** in his cell in the United Nations Detention Unit at Scheveningen, The Hague, Netherlands. He had been on trial before the **International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY)** since 12 February 2002 on **66 counts** of genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes arising from the wars in **Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Kosovo**. A post-mortem examination by the Dutch forensic institute, corroborated by the Tribunal's own inquiry, determined that he had died of a **heart attack (myocardial infarction)** from long-standing cardiovascular disease. Because Milošević died before a verdict could be reached, the proceedings were formally **terminated on 14 March 2006** with no judgment—leaving the four-year trial, one of the most important international war-crimes cases since Nuremberg, unfinished. His death, roughly three weeks before his defence case was scheduled to conclude, denied victims a legal reckoning and became a lasting emblem of the difficulty of holding heads of state accountable for atrocity crimes.

## Political Career

Slobodan Milošević rose through the Serbian Communist establishment in the 1980s, harnessing Serbian nationalism—most visibly in his 1989 speech at Gazimestan marking the 600th anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo—to consolidate power. He became **President of Serbia in 1991** and, after the breakup of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia, **President of the Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro) in 1997**. Across the 1990s he was the dominant political figure in Belgrade during the wars that accompanied Yugoslavia's disintegration—the **Croatian War of Independence (1991–1995)**, the **Bosnian War (1992–1995)**, and the **Kosovo War (1998–1999)**—conflicts marked by ethnic cleansing, mass displacement, and the 1995 Srebrenica genocide.

Milošević's rule ended after the disputed federal elections of September 2000, when mass protests—the "Bulldozer Revolution" of **5 October 2000**—forced him from office. He was arrested by Serbian authorities in **April 2001** on domestic corruption and abuse-of-power charges.

## Path to The Hague

The ICTY had indicted Milošević in **May 1999**, while the Kosovo War was still under way, making him the **first sitting head of state to be indicted by an international tribunal**. On **28 June 2001**, the Serbian government of Prime Minister Zoran Đinđić transferred him to the custody of the ICTY in The Hague, a controversial handover that Đinđić linked to international reconstruction aid. The prosecution ultimately consolidated three indictments—covering **Kosovo, Croatia, and Bosnia**—into a single case, **IT-02-54**, comprising **66 counts**. The Bosnia indictment included a charge of **genocide** for the events of the 1992–1995 war, including Srebrenica.

## The ICTY Trial

The trial opened on **12 February 2002**. Milošević refused to recognise the Tribunal's legitimacy and **conducted his own defence**, declining assigned counsel and using the proceedings as a political platform. The prosecution called some 300 witnesses over roughly two years, presenting evidence intended to establish his command responsibility and participation in a "joint criminal enterprise" spanning the Yugoslav wars. Proceedings were repeatedly delayed by Milošević's ill health—chronic high blood pressure and heart disease—which caused frequent adjournments. His defence phase, which began in 2004, was nearing completion at the time of his death; the Tribunal estimated that only about three weeks of his case remained.

## The 2006 Death

On the morning of **11 March 2006**, Milošević was found dead in his cell at the Scheveningen detention unit. In the days before his death he had petitioned the Tribunal for provisional release to receive specialised cardiac treatment at a clinic in **Moscow**, a request the judges rejected on 23 February 2006 out of concern he would not return. He had also written a letter to the Russian Foreign Ministry claiming he was being poisoned—an assertion he tied to the presence of an unprescribed drug detected in earlier blood tests.

A **post-mortem examination** conducted by the **Netherlands Forensic Institute** on 12 March 2006 concluded that Milošević had died of a **myocardial infarction**. The Tribunal noted that he had suffered from serious cardiovascular disease and stated that he had at times **declined to take the heart medication prescribed to him**, medicating himself instead.

## The Parker Inquiry

To address the poisoning allegations and questions about his medical care, ICTY President Fausto Pocar appointed the Tribunal's Vice-President, Judge **Kevin Parker**, to lead an independent inquiry. The **Parker Report**, published on **30 May 2006**, concluded that Milošević died of **natural causes** and found **no poison or other chemical substance in his body that contributed to the death**. It confirmed that an unprescribed drug (rifampicin) had earlier been found in his system but determined it did not cause his death, while noting that his self-medication and refusal of prescribed treatment had complicated management of his condition.

## Reaction and Legacy

Milošević's death produced sharply divided reactions. Nationalist supporters in Serbia mourned him and voiced suspicion that the Tribunal bore responsibility, while critics and many victims' groups expressed frustration that he had **escaped a verdict**. Serbia's government declined to grant him a state funeral; he was buried on 18 March 2006 in the garden of his family home in Požarevac after a large gathering of supporters in Belgrade.

For the ICTY, the death was a profound setback. The Milošević case had been the Tribunal's flagship prosecution, and its termination without judgment meant that the fullest legal accounting of Belgrade's role in the Yugoslav wars would never be rendered against its central figure—although the evidentiary record it produced informed later cases, including those against Radovan Karadžić and Ratko Mladić.

## Significance

The death of Slobodan Milošević in 2006 marked the abrupt, inconclusive end of the most ambitious international war-crimes trial since the post-World War II tribunals. As the first sitting head of state indicted by an international court, his prosecution had been a landmark test of the principle that heads of state are not immune from accountability for genocide and crimes against humanity; his death before verdict exposed the fragility of that principle when justice moves slowly. The episode unfolded in the same year that the [State Union of Serbia and Montenegro dissolved]({{ '/news/history-politics/montenegro-independence/' | relative_url }})—the final unwinding of the Yugoslav federation Milošević had once dominated—and joined a striking cluster of 2006 endings for authoritarian leaders facing accountability, alongside the deaths of [Augusto Pinochet]({{ '/news/people/augusto-pinochet/' | relative_url }}) and the [execution of Saddam Hussein]({{ '/news/history-politics/saddam-hussein-execution/' | relative_url }}). Together these cases framed enduring debates over international justice: whether tribunals can deliver timely accountability, and what is lost when a defendant dies before judgment.
