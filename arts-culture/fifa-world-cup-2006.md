# 2006 FIFA World Cup Final

**Category**: Arts & Culture
**Key figures**: Zinedine Zidane and Marco Materazzi (players); Marcello Lippi (Italy manager); Horacio Elizondo (referee)

## Summary

The **2006 FIFA World Cup** (the nineteenth edition of the tournament) was hosted by **Germany** from **June 9 to July 9, 2006**, and played across **12 host cities** including Berlin, Munich, Frankfurt, and Dortmund. Thirty-two teams competed across a group stage, round of 16, quarter-finals, semi-finals, and final. The tournament attracted an aggregate live attendance of **3,359,439** across 64 matches — an average of 52,491 per game, the highest recorded average attendance for any World Cup to that date. The global television audience was estimated at **26.29 billion** cumulative viewers across all matches.

The final, played on **July 9, 2006**, at the **Olympiastadion** in Berlin before approximately **69,000 spectators**, pitted **Italy** against **France**. Italy won on penalties after a **1–1 draw** through 120 minutes, claiming its fourth World Cup title and its first in 24 years. The match is most remembered for a moment in the 110th minute when French captain **Zinedine Zidane** — playing the final match of his career — headbutted Italian defender **Marco Materazzi** in the chest and was sent off by referee **Horacio Elizondo**, an image that became one of the most replayed in football history.

## Tournament Overview

### Host Selection and Infrastructure

Germany was awarded hosting rights at the FIFA Executive Committee meeting in **Zurich on July 6, 2000**, defeating South Africa by **12 votes to 11** in a result that later attracted controversy: a FIFA investigation in 2016 found evidence that a **$6.7 million payment** had been routed through FIFA accounts before the vote in a manner suggesting votes may have been purchased, though the conclusion was disputed. The 2006 tournament proceeded without incident.

Germany constructed or substantially refurbished ten stadiums for the tournament, with the centerpiece being the **Allianz Arena** in Munich (capacity 69,901) and the renovated **Olympiastadion** in Berlin (capacity 74,228 for the final). The German government invested approximately **€1.4 billion** in infrastructure improvements, including high-speed rail connections between host cities.

The tournament's official anthem was **"Time of Our Lives"** performed by Il Divo and Toni Braxton, and Shakira performed **"Hips Don't Lie"** at both the opening ceremony at the Allianz Arena on June 9 and the closing ceremony at the Olympiastadion on July 9 — see [Shakira's U.S. Breakthrough](shakira-2006-music-breakthrough.md).

### Key Results and Storylines

**Germany** as host exceeded expectations: managed by **Jürgen Klinsmann**, they defeated Australia (4–0), Poland (1–0), Ecuador (3–0), and Sweden (2–0) before losing to Italy in the semi-finals (0–2), then defeating Portugal 3–1 in the third-place play-off. German striker **Miroslav Klose** won the Golden Boot with **5 goals**.

**Brazil**, entering as favorites, were eliminated in the quarter-finals by France in a surprisingly one-sided match (0–1), ending their hopes of a sixth World Cup title. The result demonstrated that Brazil's star-studded but aging squad (Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, Roberto Carlos) was past its peak.

**Host nation effects**: Germany's unexpected run to the semi-finals produced a national atmosphere of collective celebration — coined "Sommermärchen" (summer fairy tale) — that was broadly credited with reviving German public confidence and national pride in a way that bridged the country's historically complicated relationship with national expression.

## The Final: Italy vs. France

### Match Narrative

France took an early lead in the **7th minute** when Zidane converted a penalty with a delicate chipped "Panenka" — a technique named after Czechoslovak player Antonín Panenka who had used it to win the 1976 UEFA European Championship — that struck the underside of the crossbar and crossed the line. Italy equalized in the **19th minute** when Marco Materazzi headed in a corner taken by **Andrea Pirlo**. The score remained 1–1 through the end of regulation and all of extra time.

### The Zidane Headbutt

In the **110th minute**, with approximately ten minutes remaining in extra time, Zinedine Zidane — who had announced his retirement before the tournament, making this his career-final match — suddenly turned and headbutted Materazzi in the chest. Materazzi fell to the ground. Elizondo consulted with the fourth official (who had seen the incident on his monitor) and showed Zidane a **red card**, ending the match career of France's greatest player.

The exchange that preceded the headbutt became one of sport's most analyzed incidents. Lip-readers retained by media organizations interpreted Materazzi as making a comment about Zidane's sister; Materazzi subsequently confirmed he had made a crude remark, and Zidane confirmed it was about a family member. In a television interview days later, Zidane called the headbutt "inexcusable" but said he could not regret the action given what was said, adding that dying is preferable to delivering such an insult.

Despite being dismissed, Zidane was awarded the **Golden Ball** as the tournament's best player — a vote that had largely concluded before the final — reflecting the quality of his tournament performances, particularly his magnificent quarter-final volley against Portugal and his general leadership of the French team.

## Italy's Campaign and the Calciopoli Backdrop

Italy's tournament run unfolded against a dramatic domestic backdrop: in May 2006, Italian football was shaken by **Calciopoli** (*calcio* = football + *polizia* = police), a wiretap-based investigation revealing that club directors had improperly communicated with referee associations to influence match official appointments. **Juventus** was relegated to Serie B and stripped of two league titles; AC Milan and other clubs received points deductions. Several Italian players in the World Cup squad were members of clubs implicated in the scandal.

Italy won the World Cup with a disciplined defensive approach under Lippi — conceding only **2 goals** in normal play throughout the tournament — and the victory was widely interpreted within Italy as a source of national redemption amid domestic scandal. The team that triumphed in Berlin was anchored by experienced Serie A professionals: goalkeeper **Gianluigi Buffon**, defenders **Fabio Cannavaro** (awarded the **Ballon d'Or** for 2006 — one of the few defenders ever to win the prize) and **Alessandro Nesta**, midfielder **Pirlo**, and forwards **Francesco Totti** and **Luca Toni** (who top-scored for Italy with 2 goals).

Cannavaro was awarded the FIFA World Player of the Year and Ballon d'Or for 2006, becoming the first defender to win the Ballon d'Or since Germany's Franz Beckenbauer in 1976.

## Significance

As the championship match of the world's most-watched sporting event, the 2006 final was followed by a global television audience numbering in the hundreds of millions, and Italy's victory was a landmark moment in Italian sporting history. The Zidane–Materazzi headbutt became one of the most replayed and discussed images in football history — an unexpectedly dramatic coda to the career of one of the sport's greatest players and a lasting symbol of the fine line between brilliance and self-destruction in elite competition.

The tournament also demonstrated Germany's organizational excellence and its capacity to host major international events with efficiency and good-natured pride. The "Sommermärchen" atmosphere prompted significant discussion about the evolution of German national identity — a debate that the German football team's subsequent success (reaching the 2010 semi-finals, winning the 2014 World Cup) would continue.
